# Maintenance Retainers

The optional monthly relationship that follows a one-time build. Done right, retainers are the most stable revenue a freelancer can have. Done wrong, they're scope-creep with a recurring invoice.

---

## When to read this

User says:
- "How do I offer ongoing maintenance?"
- "The client wants me to keep updating it"
- "Set up a retainer"
- "Monthly site care"

---

## When to offer a retainer

The natural moment is **near the end of the 30-day post-launch support window** (`07_handoff_and_training.md`). The client has seen the site work, has discovered things they want to change, and is now thinking about who handles it ongoing.

Don't offer a retainer:
- Before the project has shipped
- If the build went badly (the client doesn't want more time with you)
- To clients who edit the site themselves regularly and like that ("I'll just hop into GitHub and fix it")
- To clients who haven't paid the final invoice (warning sign)

Offer a retainer:
- When the client asks "how do I get this updated when I need to?"
- When the client has a regular content cadence (monthly product launches, weekly blog posts) that they don't want to manage
- When their site has moving parts (payments, integrations) that need monitoring
- When you have time and the client is good

## Tier shapes

Three tiers, low-medium-high (same anchoring pattern as `04_pricing.md` project packages). Pick reasonable numbers for your market and adjust.

### Care — $150–$300/month

**For**: clients who edit the site themselves and just want backup if something breaks.

**Includes:**
- Monthly check (uptime, broken links, form submissions arriving, payment processor working)
- Up to 1 hour of small text/image fixes
- Software updates (renewing certificates, checking third-party services haven't broken)
- 48-hour email response

**Excludes:**
- New pages or features
- Design changes
- Anything taking more than ~30 minutes per request

This is mostly insurance. The client pays so they have someone to call when things go sideways.

### Updates — $400–$800/month

**For**: clients who want regular content / small additions without paying per-request.

**Includes:**
- Everything in Care
- Up to 4 hours of edits per month (any combination of text, photos, page tweaks, small features)
- 24-hour email response
- Quarterly review (we look at analytics, talk about what's working)

**Excludes:**
- Major redesigns or significant new sections
- New integrations (adding Stripe products, swapping email providers)

This is the most popular tier in practice. The 4-hour budget is enough for typical "Sara wants three product photos updated" requests.

### Growth — $1,000–$2,500/month

**For**: clients with high site activity (frequent launches, regular promotions, active blog/content).

**Includes:**
- Everything in Updates
- 8–12 hours of work per month
- Priority response (within hours, not days)
- Monthly strategy call (15 min)
- Light SEO maintenance, analytics review

This tier is more like a part-time fractional engagement. Best for clients with a recurring marketing rhythm.

## What's IN vs. OUT — write it down

The single biggest mistake: vague retainer scope. Spell it out in the retainer agreement.

```
What this retainer covers:
  - Text edits, image swaps, link updates
  - Small layout adjustments to existing pages
  - Minor styling tweaks (color, spacing, fonts)
  - Quarterly performance/accessibility re-audit
  - Monitoring + responding to third-party service issues

What this retainer DOES NOT cover (billable separately):
  - New pages or page templates
  - New features or integrations (CMS, payment processor swap, etc.)
  - Major redesigns
  - Content writing (copy, photography, video)
  - Marketing strategy, SEO consulting, ad management

Hours roll over within the calendar quarter but not beyond.
Unused hours expire on the last day of each quarter.
```

The "do not cover" list is the important one. Without it, retainers turn into "free additional work" in the client's mind.

## Quarterly cadence beats monthly

Retainers built around month-by-month hour tracking are exhausting for both sides. Better pattern:

- **Quarterly hour pool**: client pays monthly, but you allocate hours by quarter (12 hours/quarter instead of 4/month).
- **Roll-over within the quarter only**: if the client uses 1 hour in April, 1 in May, they have 10 left for June. But unused June hours don't carry to July.
- **End-of-quarter brief report**: 1-page email — what was done, what's working, what to think about next quarter.

This pattern matches how clients actually think (they want something done *that week*, not "exactly 4 hours per month"), and it smooths out lumpy demand.

## Billing the retainer

Set up a **recurring monthly invoice** in whatever tool you use (Stripe Invoicing, Wave, Bonsai — see `05_invoicing_and_payment.md`). Net-7 payment terms.

```
Invoice #: 2026-RET-001
Date: 2026-06-01
Due: 2026-06-08

Oakmoss Studio — Updates retainer (June 2026)         $600.00

Hours used last month: 3 of 4
Hours used this quarter so far: 3 of 12

Total                                                 $600.00
```

Two reasons to send a real monthly invoice instead of auto-charging silently:
1. The client sees what they're paying for.
2. They see the hours used — keeps them honest about asking for things efficiently.

## The retainer agreement

A short addendum to (or replacement of) the original contract. Include:

- Tier name + monthly fee
- What's in / out (the lists above)
- Hour pool + rollover policy
- Response time commitment
- Payment cadence (1st of the month, net-7)
- Termination notice (30 days either way)
- Hourly rate for billable work outside the retainer (typically $100–$200/hour for a freelancer who's running retainers)

One page, signed. Use the same template approach as `03_contracts.md`.

## When to drop a retainer

Some clients become net negatives over time. Signs:

- Constant requests right at the edge of "is this in scope?"
- Multiple stakeholders fighting over what to ask for
- Communication exploding (Slacks at midnight, urgent texts)
- Hour budget consistently blown — and they push back on the overage invoice
- Tone has soured

Give 30 days' notice (per the agreement) and walk. Replace with a better client. The reason retainers compound to financial stability is because the relationships compound — but a bad retainer can offset two good ones.

## Pricing the retainer relative to the project

Rough rule of thumb: **monthly retainer ≈ 10–20% of the original project fee**.

Examples:
- $5,000 project → $500–$1,000/month retainer
- $10,000 project → $1,000–$2,000/month retainer

This isn't a formula, it's a sanity check. The actual price comes from how much time the client will eat each month + what they value the relationship at.

## How to pitch it

End of the support window, ~3 weeks after launch:

```
Hi Sara,

Quick note as we wrap up the 30-day post-launch window.

If you want ongoing care for the site, here are the options:

  Care     — $200/mo — monitoring + 1 hr of fixes
  Updates  — $600/mo — 4 hrs/mo for edits + content updates
  Growth   — $1,500/mo — 10 hrs + monthly strategy call

About half of my clients keep me on Updates; some don't need
anything and we go to per-issue. Both are totally fine.

If you want to chat through which makes sense, happy to. Otherwise,
after [date] I'll switch to per-issue billing at $150/hr.

— Jane
```

Frame it as their choice between three reasonable options + an opt-out (per-issue). Don't pressure.

## Common pitfalls

- ❌ Offering a retainer without writing down what's in/out. Guaranteed scope creep within two months.
- ❌ Tracking hours invisibly. The client should always know where the budget stands.
- ❌ Letting hours roll over indefinitely. By month 6 the client has 24 banked hours and asks you to redesign the site.
- ❌ Pricing the retainer too low because you "want the recurring revenue." A bad retainer eats time at break-even. Better to have no retainer than a money-losing one.
- ❌ Selling a $1500/mo Growth retainer to a small business that doesn't have that activity. They'll churn in 3 months.
- ❌ Continuing a retainer past its expiration date. If a client's needs have dropped, scale them down (or off) proactively — protects the relationship.
