# Client Intake

The conversation **before** you write a proposal. Goal: figure out whether this is a project you should take, and if so, what it actually is. Bad intake leads to scope creep, unpaid revisions, and clients who think you're staff.

---

## When to read this

User says:
- "Someone wants me to build them a site — what do I ask?"
- "I have a call with a potential client"
- "How do I figure out what they actually want?"
- "Help me write an intake form"

---

## The 20-minute first call

The first conversation should be short and structured. If it runs over an hour, the project is too big or the client is rambling — both signals.

### Five questions, in order

1. **"What's the business / project, in one sentence?"**
   - Watch for: clarity vs. word soup. A clear answer means they know what they're selling.

2. **"What does success look like in six months?"**
   - Watch for: outcomes vs. features. "We're getting 5 leads a week" is great. "We have a beautiful site" is a yellow flag — beauty is unmeasurable.

3. **"Do you have a deadline, and if so, why that date?"**
   - Watch for: real deadlines (trade show, product launch, lease starts) vs. arbitrary ones ("ASAP"). Arbitrary deadlines compress your work for no reason.

4. **"Have you worked with a designer / developer before? How did it go?"**
   - Watch for: how they describe the previous person. If everyone they've worked with was "terrible," the constant is them.

5. **"What's your budget range?"**
   - Watch for: deflection ("how much do you usually charge?"). You can give a range back (see `04_pricing.md`), but you need a number from them eventually. "We don't have a budget yet" usually means under $500.

---

## Qualifying questions (ask if the first five go well)

- Who else is involved in deciding? (Are they the decision-maker, or do they need their partner / board to approve?)
- What content do you have ready, and what still needs to be written?
- Do you own the domain / brand assets / photos?
- Who will maintain the site after launch — you or me?
- Have you priced any other quotes? (Anchors expectations.)

---

## Red flags (decline or scope-down)

| Signal | What it usually means |
|---|---|
| "We don't have a budget yet — what do you usually charge?" | They want a number before they share theirs; they'll lowball or vanish |
| "It's a really quick job, should only take a few hours" | Anyone who tells you how long the work will take *is* the work |
| "We've been burned before by developers" | Sometimes legit, often a control issue — ask for specifics |
| "We need it by [date 3 days away]" | Either fake urgency or genuinely impossible. Both bad. |
| Multiple stakeholders, none clearly in charge | Design-by-committee. Triple the timeline; double the price. |
| "Can you also do [SEO / ads / branding / app / video]?" | Scope creep before the contract is even drafted |
| Family / close-friend pressure to discount | The discount becomes a permanent expectation across the rest of their network |
| They want to pay in equity / exposure | Decline. Doesn't pay rent. |
| They refuse to do a contract | Decline, immediately |

You don't have to take every project. Most freelancer regret traces to a project they should have declined.

---

## Green flags (a project worth quoting)

- Specific goal tied to a number (revenue, leads, sign-ups)
- One clear decision-maker
- Realistic timeline with a real reason behind it
- Comfortable answering "what's the budget?"
- Has done the work to come prepared (logo, basic copy, examples they like)
- "We want to launch in 6 weeks, then run with it ourselves" (clean exit)

## Intake form template

If you don't want to do the questions live, send this **before** the call. Saves time. Markdown / Google Form / Notion / Tally / anything that produces a one-page response.

```markdown
# Project Intake — <your business>

Thanks for reaching out. A few quick questions before we hop on a call.
Most people fill this in 5 minutes.

**Business / project name**

**One-sentence pitch for what you do**

**Your website (if any) and any sites you like the look or feel of**

**Goal for the new site (e.g., generate leads, sell a product, take bookings)**

**Deadline, if any (and why?)**

**Approximate budget range**
  ☐ Under $1,000
  ☐ $1,000 – $3,000
  ☐ $3,000 – $7,000
  ☐ $7,000+

**Have you worked with a designer / developer before?**

**Anything else I should know?**
```

Embed it on your portfolio site (`09_finding_clients.md`) or send it as a link.

## After the call: write it down

Within 24 hours, send a follow-up email that summarizes:

```
Thanks for the call. Here's what I heard:

- You want <X> in order to <Y>
- Launch target: <date>
- Approximate budget: <range>
- Decision-maker: <name>
- Next step: I'll send a proposal by <date>

Did I miss anything?
```

This pins down their understanding before you write the proposal. If they correct anything, you find out **now**, not after you've quoted.

---

## When to walk

If after the call you're unsure, you're allowed to say:

> "I appreciate you considering me. Based on what we discussed, this isn't the right fit for me right now — but I can recommend [colleague] who might be a better match."

Two ways this helps:
1. You avoid a bad project.
2. Your network remembers you sent them business.

You only have so many client slots. Pick well.

---

## Common pitfalls

- ❌ Quoting on the first call. Always sleep on it. Send the proposal 1–3 business days later.
- ❌ Going wide on questions ("tell me everything about your business!"). Five well-placed questions beat 30 generic ones.
- ❌ Skipping the budget question because it feels rude. It's not rude. Skipping it wastes both of your time.
- ❌ Confusing enthusiasm with fit. A client who loves you on the call can still be a bad project.
